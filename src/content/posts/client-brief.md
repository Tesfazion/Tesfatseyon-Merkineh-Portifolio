Nearly every revision request I've ever been sent could have been
prevented by one question at the start. Not a brief. Not a form. One
question, asked before I touch the timeline.

**What should happen after someone watches this?**

That's it. Not "what's the vibe" or "can we make it punchier" — those
are descriptions of a symptom. What should *happen*.

## Why it works

Because "make it punchier" has about forty different meanings and none
of them are measurable. When a client says that and I don't ask, I pick
one interpretation, cut for two hours, deliver it, and get asked to
change it. And the version they wanted was one of the other thirty-nine.

The goal question collapses that. "People should watch to the end
because they want to see what I do next" is a completely different edit
from "people should watch to the end because they want to hear the joke."
Same footage. Same client. Opposite timelines, and I can build either one
in an afternoon if I know which one it is on day one.

It also settles the things that otherwise come back as revisions late:

- How long should it be, really?
- Should the product appear, and if so when?
- Is text on screen part of this or not?
- What does the viewer need to know in the first two seconds?

None of those are judgement calls I'd want to make alone. They're cheap
to ask and expensive to guess.

## The re-edit is a conversation failure

Here's the part I had to stop defending myself about. When a revision
request feels unreasonable, my instinct is still to think I'm being
picky about creative decisions. It usually isn't.

If the client has to re-explain their goal after the edit, the goal
wasn't established. That's on me, regardless of how reasonable the
revision sounded in isolation.

The re-edit isn't a complaint about my work. It's the first honest
statement of the brief, arriving too late.

## What I do instead now

Before the first cut, I send back a short written summary — four or five
lines — of what I heard: the goal, the length, the platform, the tone,
and the one thing that must be true when they watch it to the end. I ask
them to correct it.

It takes me ten minutes. It has removed essentially all the re-edits I
used to get, and several of the disagreements I used to have. Nobody has
ever once sent back a summary I'd written about their own project and
said "that's not what I meant" — but plenty of people have read one and
added a line I hadn't thought of, which is the whole point.

## The same question works in code

I use an equivalent on the web side, and it functions the same way. Before
building anything I ask what has to be true when it's done, in a form
that can be checked. Not "make it modern" but "a new visitor on a phone
should get to the contact form without scrolling."

It sounds obvious written down like that. In practice, "make it modern"
is the web equivalent of "make it punchier," and it's the reason projects
run long.

Ask the question that makes the work checkable. Then the work is
checkable, and the revision conversation mostly disappears — not because
you got it right first time, but because you found out what right meant
before you'd spent the budget.
