Most of the short-form work I get is footage shot in 16:9. It goes out
in 9:16. That is a genuinely different problem from editing, and treating
it as a crop is why so much repurposed footage looks wrong.

A 16:9 frame is 1.78 units wide. A 9:16 frame is 0.56. You are throwing
away **two thirds of the width**. Everything the shot is actually about
may be in the part you're discarding, and you often find that out after
the edit is finished.

## The maths is the whole problem

```
16:9  ->  9:16 by cropping width  = lose ~69% of the frame
16:9  ->  1:1  by cropping width  = lose ~44% of the frame
```

The more you crop, the less the framing decisions that were made at
shooting time mean anything. A two-shot becomes a close-up on whichever
person was nearer the centre. An establishing shot of a room becomes a
sliver. A product on a table disappears.

This is why a horizontal clip that "should be easy" is often the hardest
one in the batch.

## What I do before I cut anything

**Watch it vertical and write down what breaks.** Frame by frame, on the
first pass. I get three lists: shots that work as-is, shots that need a
different centre, and shots that are simply unusable. Knowing the third
list exists is the whole value — it's usually one or two shots, and
they're often the establishing shot that everything else was cut around.

**Never auto-reframe blindly.** Plenty of tools will track a face and
reframe automatically, and for talking-head content it genuinely works.
For anything with two people, a product, or a wide shot, tracking picks
whichever face is more central and confidently cuts the other person out
of the video, which is a much worse outcome than a static crop.

**Decide the centre per shot, not per video.** The mistake here is
setting one crop for the whole clip. A two-shot and a wide shot almost
never want the same centre, and the "safe" centre that's correct for the
wide shot is usually wrong for the close one.

**Move the frame, don't move the subject.** If the framing is wrong, the
cheapest fix is usually a cut — go to a different shot at that moment
rather than trying to make a bad one work.

## When to rebuild the shot instead

Sometimes the right answer is to re-shoot, or to rebuild the layout in
the edit rather than crop it. If the content genuinely needs the width —
a product, a screen recording, a wide interaction — then no crop saves
it.

The options in rough order of preference:

1. Use a shot that already works vertically.
2. Re-crop, centring deliberately, accepting some loss.
3. Rebuild the element as separate layers over a new background.
4. Re-shoot natively vertical.

Most of the time the answer is 1, and it takes a second to notice. The
mistake is committing to 2 before looking for 1.

## The thing worth saying out loud

Horizontal-to-vertical is not a format conversion. It's a re-edit, and
it should be scoped and paid for as one.

If a client wants eight clips from a piece shot for YouTube, that's not
"crop and export." That's picking the eight moments that survive
vertically, re-cutting them for a different pacing, and checking each one
on a phone with the sound off. It takes a few times longer than the
alternative, and the alternative is the version that doesn't hold
attention — which is the only thing the format is for.
