I get the same question from people reviewing my [work](/web): *where are
the big brand names?* The honest answer is that I don't have them yet, and
padding the page with logos I don't work with would have been easy and
worthless.

So I built the page around what I can actually stand behind.

## The problem with a client roster

A logo wall looks like evidence and isn't. The client never agreed to be a
badge, the logo says nothing about the work, and the person reading it cannot
tell whether you shipped a landing page or maintained a checkout flow for
three years.

Testimonials have the same problem, plus one more: they are unverifiable.
A single sentence from a name with no face attached proves that a sentence
was written.

## What I used instead

Four things, and all of them are checkable:

1. **A repository, per project.** Not one good repo — one per project, so
   the structure is visible and the commit history is honest about how long
   it actually took.
2. **A written case study.** What the problem was, what I chose, what
   happened. The [project modals](/web) open on this rather than on a
   screenshot.
3. **The deployment.** Where it is allowed to be public, it is public. If it
   is not up, the card says so instead of linking to a dead demo.
4. **The constraint.** Every case study names what I would do differently.
   A page with no weaknesses in it reads as unedited.

## Why the constraints mattered most

The reflection section was the part I nearly cut, and it is the part that
did the most work. Clients do not only check whether you can do the thing —
they check how you talk about the things that went wrong. Someone who can
describe their own mistake precisely is someone whose estimate you can
trust.

> A portfolio is a sales page. The only rule I care about is that every
> sentence on it survives a follow-up question.

I would rather arrive at a first call with a short page and no borrowed
credibility than a long page I have to apologise for. The work is public.
Let it argue for itself.
