---
title: "Most edits people call 'bad' are audio problems"
date: "2026-08-21"
tags: ["Video Editing", "Retention", "Craft"]
excerpt: "When a client says the edit feels amateur, the picture is almost never what they mean. It's the sound."
---

A client once told me a cut felt "cheap" and I spent an evening
re-cutting the whole thing. Tighter here, a transition there, better
music bed. It still felt cheap.

Then I exported it again with the audio properly levelled and normalised
— same timeline, same cuts, about ninety seconds of work — sent that
version instead, and they came back with the next batch.

The picture was never the problem. I want to be specific about why,
because the fix is quick and almost nobody looks for it.

## Earphones hide the problem

I edit in headphones. Everyone does. Headphones are the worst possible
monitoring setup for judging short-form, for two reasons.

**They're too clean.** Good headphones, in a quiet room, let you hear
every problem. Real viewers are on a phone speaker, on a bus, with a
notification chiming. All the subtlety you're monitoring for is gone, and
what's left is *level* — and level is what headphones make hardest to
judge, because everything sounds presentable.

**They hide the frequency range that matters.** Phone speakers can't
reproduce much below about 300 Hz, and they roll off hard at the top. A
mix balanced on headphones is typically too thin and too quiet on a
phone: the bass is doing nothing, and there's no headroom left to
compensate.

## What I actually do

**Watch it out loud, on a speaker, once per project.** Not on the
timeline — on the exported file. The first time I do this on a new
project I usually find something.

**Set an absolute target, not a relative one.** Not "the dialogue sits
about here" but a real number. Dialogue around −6 dBFS peak is a decent
place to start; the point is that a target can be checked and a feeling
can't.

**Normalise the last step, every time.** Loudness normalisation at export
is a checkbox, not a setting to experiment with. The platforms already
normalise; if your source is wildly different from the target you get
pumped or crushed on the way in.

**Check the music under the voice, not over it.** A music bed that
competes with dialogue is the most common reason a short-form edit feels
amateur, and it's obvious the instant you hear it out loud and nearly
impossible to notice on headphones.

**Leave headroom. Add nothing.** If it feels too quiet on headphones, it
is almost certainly correct for a phone.

## The captions are part of the audio

One more thing, because it sits in the same place. Captions aren't an
accessory, they're a second audio channel, and most of the "this looks
amateur" feeling from a sound-off viewer comes from captions that are
late, inconsistently styled, or missing the words that matter.

Read the captions back on their own, timed, with the video muted. If they
read as a mess or trail the speech, that is a real defect and it's
fixable in the timeline in a few minutes.

## The uncomfortable conclusion

The picture is what makes an edit look expensive. The sound is what makes
it *feel* finished. They're not equally weighted in how people respond,
which is strange, because the picture is the part that took longer and
that everyone looks at while you work.

I lost a day to a colour grade on a cut that was already fine. I lost
ninety seconds to a limiter on a cut that had been failing for a week.

Edit the picture like it matters, because it does. But check the sound
like it decides the outcome, because it does that too.
